What the original does

Instant Haus is a MIDI effect, not an instrument. It sits in front of a drum rack in Ableton Live, watches the transport, and fires notes. There is no sequencer to draw in — every track has a single Pattern knob, and turning it swaps in one of a dozen fixed 16-step figures. Kick, snare and percussion have twelve each; the hi-hat track has twenty-four, because it drives two voices (closed and open) off one grid.

Each pattern is stored as a two-row matrix: the top row is an accented hit, the bottom row a ghost. The two rows map onto the Vel Hi and Vel Lo fields, so the same figure can be flat, or nearly all ghost notes with two accents in it. The hi-hat grid has four rows — closed accent, closed ghost, open accent, open ghost — which is how one knob produces a two-voice hat line.

Swing delays every other 16th, per track, so the hats can shuffle while the kick stays straight. Shift is a plain millisecond delay on the whole track, up to 25 ms — the trick that makes a machine-tight pattern sit slightly behind the beat. Both are per track, and that is the point of the device: the groove comes from four tracks disagreeing slightly about where the beat is.

Pattern 0 is silence on every track, which is why Perc starts there.

Where the numbers come from

An .amxd is a small binary header followed by the Max patcher as JSON. The pattern banks live in it as matrixctrl messages — triplets of column row value — behind a select object, one message box per knob position. Parsing the JSON and following those patch cords gives all 96 grids, the default note numbers and the parameter ranges, with no guessing from screenshots.

Note map (OP-XY drum kit)

VoiceNoteNameIn the device

The Note field on each track is the note the track sends, and it is live: change it and the grid label, this table and the MIDI output all follow.

Notes: GM / OP-XY in the console switches the whole set at once. GM is the General MIDI percussion map the device itself ships — 36 kick, 38 snare, 42 and 46 hats — which is also where an Ableton drum rack starts. OP-XY is what a Teenage Engineering drum kit answers to: F for the kick, G for the snare, C and C# for the two hats. Picking one loads its notes straight away, and it is what Init restores and the span Randomize › Note rolls within — 35–59 for GM, 53–68 for OP-XY, so a roll lands on another drum in the same kit rather than off it. The last column is always the device's own value, for comparison.

Reading the grid

Five rows, one per voice: Kick, Snare, CH and OH off the hi-hat track, and Perc. Each column is a 16th; bold numbers are beats. A filled cell is an accent (Vel Hi), a half-lit cell a ghost (Vel Lo). Click a cell to cycle it off → ghost → accent; the pattern number then reads edit until you dial a new one.

Swing runs 50–100 % as in the original, where 50 is straight. It delays only the odd 16ths — the ‘e’ and the ‘a’ — so a figure that lives on the ‘&’, like the plain offbeat hat line, does not move at all however far you turn the knob. 66 is a triplet shuffle; past 80 the note is nearly on top of the next 16th. Shift is 0–25 ms and moves everything in the track. The original leaves the kick’s shift out of its randomiser, so there is always something to be late against.

Both are visible in the grid itself: the slot stays where the 16th is, and the block inside it slides right by shift plus, on odd steps, swing. A thin arrow runs from the block back to the 16th it belongs to, and a block pushed far enough overlaps its neighbour — which is what happens in time too. The line under the grid gives the same figures in milliseconds at the current tempo.

Randomize reproduces the device's own ranges: Patt rolls 0–11 (0–23 for hats), Swing 50–99, Shift 0–25 on everything but the kick, and Vel gives kick, snare and perc an accent of 100–126 against a ghost of 0–99, while both hat voices get a free 0–127 for either — which is why hats come back wilder than the rest. Init restores the shipped defaults.

MIDI out is not in the original — it did not need one, since Live was downstream. Enable it, pick a port, and the page sends note on/off on the chosen channel alongside its own audio, so it can drive an OP-XY, a TR-6S or a drum rack. Chromium-based browsers only; Safari and Firefox have no Web MIDI.

The kits

The original makes no sound at all — it is a note generator. These four are stand-ins, each voice a small parametric synth. Roland TR-909 — 1983, analogue kick and snare with sampled cymbals: the house kit.

707 — Roland TR-707, 1985. PCM samples rather than analogue circuits: short, crisp, a little grainy. Cheap second-hand by the late eighties, which is why it is all over early Chicago house.

808 — Roland TR-808, 1980. A sine kick that rings for most of a second, a tonal snare, hats built from six square waves. In house it usually turns up as the kick under a 909 top.

909 — Roland TR-909, 1983. Analogue kick, snare and toms, sampled hats and cymbals. The default house and techno kit, and the one Alexkid's patterns assume.

LinnDrum — Roger Linn's LM-2, 1982. Not synthesis at all: real drums recorded and played back as 8-bit samples, which is why the kick barely bends and the hats are dark and gritty. Its snare is one of the most recognisable sounds of the decade, and house producers sampled the machine long before they could afford one — so it belongs here more than it first looks.

What is not the same

No Live. The original slaves to Live's transport and inherits its tempo; this page has its own clock and a tempo slider. Its swing was scaled against the host tempo in a way that left a little shuffle in even at 50 %; here 50 % is genuinely straight.

One hit per cell. A handful of the source patterns set the accent and ghost row on the same step, which in Max fires the voice twice. The grid shows one cell per step, so those collapse to an accent.

No MIDI learn, no Assign Rack. The original's MIDI to menu and Rack button map incoming notes to its own parameters and to a drum rack's pads. Neither has anywhere to point on a web page.
