Excursion

A dub desk, not a step grid. The riddim is already programmed — the version gets made here, at the board, by muting things. Built from Messian Dread's Making Dub With Computers.

Tempo 140

Kit Fills — ch.18 Key Show

Percussion off. Perc adds the chapter 21 rack: kete on 16ths over two bars, a four-bar shaker/woodblock/cowbell rhythm — the woodblock “at two places in a bar, repeated every two bars” — and a tambourine that isn't written out at all, because he says it's there to “enhance the Hi Hat pattern”, so it's taken from the hats. Bells and vibraslap stay on buttons: “just once or twice.”

Five ways to start a dub — chapter 31

Each one restarts the riddim and performs its own mute sequence over the first bars. Take the desk back any time — your moves override it.

And breaks — chapter 18

“A break should signify an important part like the end of a chorus or verse, or it should just do what the word says: give the riddim a break.” His own riddim rolls at bar 8, breaks at 16, crashes at 40, moves the snare at 64, pulls the hats at 79 and marks the last section at 113 — the Fills row up top fires each one on the next bar. He caps it at five breaks in a track, says never reuse the same crash cymbal, and insists a hi-hat never runs through a break: “End it with an open HiHat hit that sounds during the break.”

The rules he actually states

  • Drum and bass dominant. “There should be many parts where it's just the drum and the bass, and there should be at least three little parts in your mix where drum, bass, or both will drop out.”
  • Effects are channels, not returns. Aux 1 → delay → EQ → channel 11. The delay's own feedback stays at zero; the repeats come from looping the return back in, so fader 11 is the echo.
  • Delay time is 1/3 of a bar. Not a dotted eighth. It drifts against the beat, which is the point.
  • Reverb 2 is pre-fader. Pull a channel to zero with its verb open and you hear the wet only — the dry instrument isn't there at all.
  • Mute on the wrong side of the hit. “Pressing the mute button at a skank just after you hear the actual chord gives a special effect.”
  • The skank sits on counts 2 and 4 — not on every offbeat. “The riddim or ‘skank’ follows or emphasizes the bass line by hitting a chord on every 2nd and 4th count.” At 140 BPM that is the offbeat you hear; it only looks wrong written down. The organ then bubbles around it: the same hits pasted an eighth early an octave down, and an eighth late, both softer.
  • The bass takes its cue from the chord. “On the 2nd and 4th count of every bar, use a tone which is also present at the chord.” Am is A–C–E, Em is E–G–B, and E is the note that joins them.
  • 110–190 BPM. He's blunt about the alternative: halve it to 70 and “you overcrowd your sequencer's piano roll and lose all oversight.”
